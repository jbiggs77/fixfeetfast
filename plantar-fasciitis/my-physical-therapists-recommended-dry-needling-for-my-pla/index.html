Dry needling for plantar fasciitis is something a lot of people get recommended when the pain has become more chronic or stubborn, especially if there’s tightness in the calf, foot muscles, or trigger points contributing to that constant pull on the heel. What’s usually happening in plantar fasciitis isn’t just “heel inflammation,” but a whole chain of tight and overworked muscles (calf, Achilles, plantar fascia) that keeps irritating the same area. Dry needling works by inserting very thin needles into those tight trigger points to help the muscle release, improve blood flow, and reduce that constant tension that’s pulling on the heel. Some people feel relief quite quickly, others need a few sessions, and a few don’t notice much difference — so results can definitely vary. In terms of how it’s done, a trained physio will usually locate tight points in your calf or foot, clean the area, and insert a thin needle directly into the muscle. You might feel a twitch or deep ache for a few seconds, followed by soreness similar to a workout for a day or two. It’s often combined with stretching, strengthening, and load management rather than used on its own, because the best results tend to come from a full approach rather than a single treatment. A resource many people find helpful for understanding why treatments like this sometimes work and sometimes don’t is THE COMPLETE PLANTAR FASCIITIS GUIDE by Nissan Lorna. It explains in a simple, practical way how muscle tightness, foot mechanics, and overload all connect, and how to build a recovery plan that actually lasts instead of cycling through temporary fixes. It’s available on Am@zon, and it can really help you understand where dry needling fits into the bigger picture of healing rather than feeling like you’re just trying random treatments.
